\section{Numerical Solution by Operator Splitting}\label{sec:split}

\subsection{Why the coupled system cannot be integrated directly}
\label{sec:why-split}

The system of \cref{sec:summary-equations} on the mesh of \cref{sec:geometry}
carries $2\,446\,659$ immobile degrees of freedom and $362\,468$ mobile ones ---
$2\,809\,127$ in all. Integrated as one coupled stiff system it would be
hopeless, and it is worth being precise about why, because the numbers that
follow are easy to misread as a claim that something impossible has been done.

Three properties compound. The system is \emph{stiff}: recombination and
absorption act on microseconds while the loop populations evolve over
$10^{7}\,$s, so an explicit integrator is excluded and an implicit one must
factorize a Jacobian. It is \emph{large}: a dense factorization of the coupled
Jacobian is $O(N^{3})$ with $N=2\,809\,127$, and sparsity softens but does not
rescue that --- a three-dimensional finite-element Jacobian still factorizes at
$\sim O(N^{2})$ with fill growing as $O(N^{4/3})$. And it is \emph{long}: a
march to 10\,dpa at $10^{-7}\,\mathrm{dpa\,s^{-1}}$ is $10^{8}\,$s of physical
time, over which an adaptive integrator takes of order $10^{2}$ internal steps
per unit of the slow dynamics.

The scale separation that makes the problem hard is also what makes it
tractable. The mobile species recombine, cluster and reach the absorbing
boundaries on $\tau_M\sim(k^{2}D)^{-1}$, of order microseconds at $573\,$K and
the sink densities of interest; the immobile families evolve on the dose scale.
Thirteen decades separate them. On the slow manifold the mobile species are
\emph{slaved}: writing the mobile block as
$\dot{\spvec c}_M=\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal R_M(\spvec c_M;\spvec Q)$ and the
immobile block as $\dot{\spvec Q}=\bm g(\spvec Q;\spvec c_M)$ with
$\varepsilon=\tau_M/\tau_Q\ll1$, the singular limit $\varepsilon\to0$ replaces
the first by the algebraic constraint
$\mathcal R_M(\spvec c^{\star}_M;\spvec Q)=\bm 0$. This is the
quasi-steady-state assumption, and the spatial code implements it \emph{exactly}:
its mobile solver carries no time derivative at all. \Cref{eq:block-mobile} is
not a discretization of a transient problem taken to large times; it is the
steady problem itself.

\subsection{Structure of the split}
\label{sec:split-structure}

The two blocks are advanced by a Lie--Trotter split. Over one dose interval
$[\gamma_n,\gamma_{n+1}]$ with $\Delta t=(\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_n)/G$, the scheme
alternates:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{S\arabic*.},itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Mechanics.} Solve the elastic boundary-value problem with the
      current inelastic deformation for $\bm\sigma_n(\xv)$. Constant in the
      calculations reported here, and retained for generality.
\item \textbf{Assemble local coefficients} from the current immobile state
      $\spvec Q_n(\xv)$ and $\bm\sigma_n$: the diffusion tensors, the
      efficiencies $Z_{sk,m}$ of \cref{eq:Zsk}, the sink operator of
      \cref{eq:KM}, the quadratic $\spmat R_2$, the variant weights, and the
      boundary data.
\item \textbf{Fast solve} (finite element, Newton, spatially coupled). With
      $\spvec Q_n$ \emph{fixed}, solve
      $\mathcal R_M(\spvec c^{\star}_M;\spvec Q_n)=\bm 0$ for the quasi-steady
      mobile field.
\item \textbf{Slow march} (pointwise, decoupled). At every node, integrate
      \cref{eq:block-n,eq:block-c,eq:block-q} over $\Delta t$ with the four
      mobile values frozen at $\spvec c^{\star}_M$.
\end{enumerate}

The fast step must be inside the loop. If the mobile field is
instead replayed from a previously recorded calculation, the scheme is not an
operator split at all: $\spvec c_M$ never responds to the immobile state the
march is building, so a starting state away from quasi-steady state can never
relax. Any spatially uniform seed is such a state, because it carries no
boundary layer while the true $\spvec c^{\star}_M(\xv)$ is pinned to thermal
equilibrium on every Dirichlet face.

Solver settings must be shared across compared routes. The spatial
code's default makes the mobile solve a \emph{projected} Newton iteration that
does not converge on this problem: it caps out at $53.6\%$ error in $c^{i}$.
The projection is disabled for every route reported here. Left at the default on
one side only, a route-to-route comparison measures that solver defect rather
than the physics.

\subsection{The slow step, and why the mobile field must be frozen}
\label{sec:slow}

With $\spvec c^{\star}_M(\xv)$ held fixed, the immobile equations at different
nodes are \emph{independent}. This is not an approximation introduced for speed.
The only thing coupling neighboring points is diffusion of the mobile species,
and freezing them removes that coupling by construction; a point's immobile
equations then depend on its own loop populations and on the four mobile values
pinned into its state, and on nothing else.

The step is implemented by reusing the same stiff integrator with a flag that
zeroes the four mobile derivatives \emph{after} every reaction term has been
evaluated, so the immobile equations, the conservation accumulators and $\rho_N$
all continue to read the mobile values as frozen parameters and nothing is
dropped. Each node becomes one case of an OpenMP batch integrated concurrently,
with its own solver workspace, and identical states are deduplicated before
dispatch --- a measured factor of $1.24$ at the end of the march reported here,
and considerably higher early on, when much of the domain still shares one
state.

The integrator is chosen by measurement. CVODE with backward
differentiation formulas and a dense linear solver, at
$\mathrm{rtol}=10^{-6}$, $\mathrm{atol}=10^{-20}$. Against a tight reference,
BDF with a dense or a full-band solver gives a maximum relative error of
$3.0\times10^{-4}$; a Krylov solver gives $1.7\times10^{-2}$; and the implicit
Runge--Kutta tables designed as IMEX pairs fail outright in pure implicit mode,
by four to seven orders of magnitude. The frozen system is $36$-dimensional, so
a dense factorization is $\sim1.6\times10^{4}$ flops and there is nothing to
gain from sparsity.

\subsection{Why of order $10^{6}$ ODEs per substep is tractable}
\label{sec:cost}

The slow step of the calculation in \cref{sec:results} integrates $3\,262\,212$
scalar ordinary differential equations per substep and $7.8\times10^{7}$ over
the march. That is \emph{not} one system of three million equations. It is
$72\,928$ independent systems of $36$, and the distinction is worth ten orders
of magnitude:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lr@{}}
\toprule
one implicit factorization & flops \\
\midrule
split, $72\,928\times36^{3}$ & $3.4\times10^{9}$ \\
coupled, $(90\,617\times31)^{3}$ & $2.2\times10^{19}$ \\
\midrule
ratio & $6.5\times10^{9}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Stiffness localizes the same way. Each point's system genuinely is stiff --- of
order $100$ CVODE internal steps per point per substep, which is why BDF --- but
stiffness cost scales with the dimension of the coupled \emph{block}, not with
how many independent blocks there are.

The coupling did not vanish; it moved to where it is affordable. The
tightly coupled problem still exists: it is the fast solve, $362\,468$ unknowns,
$4\,\mathrm{m}\,12\,\mathrm{s}$ measured on 24 cores, against
$1\,\mathrm{m}\,12\,\mathrm{s}$ for an entire slow sweep over all $90\,617$
points. What the split buys is \emph{frequency}. Because
\cref{eq:block-mobile} has no time derivative, the coupled problem is solved
eight times over the march rather than at every timestep. The counterfactual,
from the same measurements at five Newton iterations per solve and hence
$\sim50\,$s per linear solve:
\begin{equation*}
  \underbrace{100\ \text{internal steps}\times24\ \text{substeps}
              \div 5\ \text{steps per refactorization}}_{\approx480\ \text{factorizations}}
  \times\;50\,\mathrm{s}\;\approx\;6.7\,\mathrm{h},
\end{equation*}
for the four-species mobile block \emph{alone}, against the $29\,$min the entire
slow side of the march actually took.

Three things stack, then: freezing the mobile field removes the spatial
coupling; the coupled part has no time derivative, so it is solved eight times
rather than thousands; and batch OpenMP with deduplication spreads what is left
over the available cores. The slow side is linear in node count, as this
structure predicts.

\subsection{Splitting error and its control}
\label{sec:splitting}

Freezing $\spvec c^{\star}_M$ over a substep ignores the drift of $\spvec Q$,
and hence of the sinks, within that substep. This is a first-order Lie--Trotter
error, $O(\Delta\gamma)$ per step and one-sided: with strengthening sinks the
loops are slightly over-grown.

The accuracy dial is the fast-solve cadence, not the dose-snapshot
spacing. The march advances the immobile block in $n_{\mathrm{sub}}$ substeps
per dose interval and re-solves the mobile field every $n_{\mathrm{fem}}$
substeps, so the splitting error is first order in the interval between fast
solves and not in the interval between output snapshots. Two practical notes
follow, both of which were established by their failure modes. The cadence
counter must run over the \emph{whole march} and not within an interval:
counted within an interval, $n_{\mathrm{fem}}$ becomes silently inoperative
whenever an interval holds fewer substeps than the cadence. And the substep
count is fixed \emph{per dose interval} rather than by a wall-clock cap on
$\Delta t$: a 5\,dpa interval at $10^{-7}\,\mathrm{dpa\,s^{-1}}$ is
$5\times10^{7}\,$s, which a fixed $5\times10^{4}\,$s cap would have split into a
thousand batch subprocesses.

Three refinements are available and are not used in the calculations reported
here. A step cap accepts a step only if
$\max_q\Delta\langle k^{2}\rangle/\langle k^{2}\rangle\le\eta_{\mathrm{split}}$
and halves $\Delta\gamma$ otherwise. Strang splitting --- half an immobile step,
a mobile re-solve, half an immobile step --- gives $O(\Delta\gamma^{2})$ for one
extra fast solve. An in-step Picard iteration between S3 and S4 removes the
splitting error entirely and is the natural fallback if a regime requires it.

\subsection{Measured cost}
\label{sec:cost-measured}

\Cref{tab:cost} reports the calculation of \cref{sec:results} on 24 cores. Two
readings matter. The fast and slow sides cost nearly the same, $52\%$ against
$45\%$, which is the balance the cadence $n_{\mathrm{fem}}=n_{\mathrm{sub}}=3$
was chosen to produce: raising the cadence buys accuracy at close to linear cost
in the fast side alone. And a single point-integration costs
$994\,\mu\mathrm s$ for $36$ stiff equations over a dose interval, which is what
makes a million of them per substep an hour of wall clock rather than a week.

\subsection{A point can fail, and where}
\label{sec:failure}

The pointwise structure makes failure local, which is a diagnostic advantage
worth stating. On an earlier anisotropic march on this geometry a single node
failed the immobile integration at one substep, and failed
\emph{deterministically} --- in the full batch, again when re-run alone, twice,
across two separate invocations. It sat $51\,b$ from the basal-pole face, inside
the boundary layer, with entirely unremarkable concentrations: 30th to 50th
percentile in every mobile species. That location is the diagnosis. Anisotropic
interstitial diffusion narrows the boundary layer along the $c$ axis and
steepens its gradient, which puts the point beyond the integrator's reach at
$\mathrm{rtol}=10^{-6}$.

Retrying such a point alone distinguishes a transient from a real failure and
costs a handful of cases against tens of thousands, but it does not recover a
point that fails alone: that needs a solver or a model answer. The available
fallback substitutes an identity step, freezing the point for that substep; it
should be understood for what it is, which is declaring the march successful
without that point rather than solving it. No point failed in the calculation
reported here.
