\title{Parallel optimization of the DisloCluster coupled march\\
       \large Five fixes, three falsified hypotheses, two retractions,
       and a $1.92\times$ that is bit-identical}
\author{DisloCluster development note}
\date{2 September 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The coupled march spends its time in two solvers: a \emph{slow} step
integrating independent stiff ODEs at every mesh node (ZrMicro, OpenMP batch)
and a \emph{fast} step solving a steady reaction--diffusion FE problem
(MoDELib3 \texttt{DDomp}). Neither was using the machine, and in \emph{every}
case the reason was not the one the phase labels suggested. Five changes follow
from measurement --- sharding the slow batch across processes, removing a token
round trip that consumed 47\,\% of the slow step, caching the FE sparsity
pattern across Newton iterations, correcting the fast step's thread count, and
making \texttt{DDomp} resident so it stops factorizing the same matrix 64 times
--- and every one is verified \textbf{bit-identical} against the unoptimized
code. On a \SI{200}{\nano\meter} hexagonal march to \SI{1}{dpa} the wall time
falls \SI{161.7}{\second} $\rightarrow$ \SI{84.1}{\second}, a factor
$\mathbf{1.92}$, with the slow step $2.39\times$ and a steady-state fast solve
$2.32\times$. Two claims made in earlier revisions of this note were wrong and
are retracted in \S\ref{sec:retract} and \S\ref{sec:retract2}; both were
corrected by profiling rather than by argument, which is the main methodological
point of the document.
\end{abstract}

\section{The machine, and why it matters}
All measurements are on a dual-socket Intel Xeon Gold 6230: \textbf{40 physical
cores, 80 logical threads}, Windows 10, with the 3-D solver under WSL2.

Windows partitions 80 logical processors into \emph{two processor groups} of 40,
and a process is confined to one unless it calls the group-affinity API:

\begin{lstlisting}
System.Environment.ProcessorCount  ->  40      (not 80)
process affinity mask              ->  1099511627775 = 2^40 - 1
\end{lstlisting}

So one process can address at most half the machine, and asking for 80 threads
oversubscribes 40 logical processors two-to-one.

\section{The slow step}

\subsection{Thread scaling: measured}
The slow step is embarrassingly parallel by construction --- freezing the mobile
species removes the only spatial coupling, leaving $N$ independent
19-dimensional systems with no neighbour state. Threads stopped paying almost
immediately. Fixed work of 20\,000 cases, three passes, machine idle:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\toprule
processes & threads & total & cases/s & speedup\\
\midrule
1 & 1 & 1 & 1\,544 & $1.00\times$\\
1 & 8 & 8 & 4\,541 & $2.94\times$\\
1 & 40 & 40 & 2\,678 & $1.73\times$\\
1 & 80 & 80 & 1\,130 & $\mathbf{0.73\times}$\\
\midrule
5 & 8 & 40 & 14\,347 & $9.29\times$\\
\rowcolor{green!12}
20 & 4 & 80 & \textbf{20\,438} & $\mathbf{13.23\times}$\\
40 & 2 & 80 & 18\,469 & $11.96\times$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At its configured 80 threads the batch ran \emph{slower than one thread}, and
the same 80 threads arranged as 20 processes of 4 are $18\times$ apart.

\subsection{Three hypotheses, all falsified}
Before changing anything, the obvious mechanisms were built and measured. All
are \textbf{ruled out}, and recording that is the point of this section:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Hypothesis & Test & Result\\
\midrule
False sharing on \texttt{status[c]}, \texttt{outbuf[c]}
  & rebuild with \texttt{schedule(dynamic,64)}
  & $1.00$--$1.01\times$\\
SUNDIALS error-handler lock
  & remove all 20\,000 messages at source
  & scaling unchanged\\
Progress text written to the pipe
  & measure the volume
  & \SI{0.69}{\mega\byte}/call, negligible\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The surviving inference is from the data: \emph{processes scale and threads do
not}, implicating a resource private to a process and shared by its threads. The
per-process heap fits (every case builds an \texttt{ostringstream} and a
\texttt{string}), but this is \textbf{not demonstrated} and is a hypothesis only.

\subsection{Fix 1: sharding}
\emph{Batching} merges work to amortize per-call overhead; \emph{sharding}
splits it to escape a per-worker ceiling. The code already batched --- all
$\sim$190\,000 cases into one process, to amortize the Windows-dominant
process-spawn cost --- which is correct but does not say when to stop.
\texttt{run\_cpp\_solver\_batch} now splits across concurrent processes, each a
self-contained batch file with its own \texttt{@BASE} header, results scattered
back by original index. Each shard is still a batch; \texttt{\_SHARD\_MIN\_CASES}
is where the two arguments balance.

At 189\,533 nodes: \SI{52.3}{\second} for the best single process against
\SI{29.2}{\second} sharded, $\mathbf{1.79\times}$, bit-identical.

\subsection{Fix 2: the token round trip}
With the solver ceiling removed, 47\,\% of the slow step was Python string
handling:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
phase & before & \% & after\\
\midrule
dedup & \SI{0.61}{\second} & 1.5 & \SI{0.72}{\second}\\
\texttt{build\_immobile\_cases} & \SI{5.31}{\second} & 13.2 & \textbf{\SI{0.00}{\second}}\\
\texttt{\_batch\_lines} & \SI{13.64}{\second} & 33.9 & \textbf{\SI{2.53}{\second}}\\
solver + parse & \SI{20.68}{\second} & 51.4 & \SI{11.46}{\second}\\
\midrule
\rowcolor{green!12}
TOTAL & \SI{39.94}{\second} & & \textbf{\SI{14.72}{\second}} $\;\mathbf{2.71\times}$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every case is the same $\sim$87-token base with 19 \texttt{y0} slots
overwritten. The builder materialized all 87 per case; \texttt{\_batch\_lines}
parsed them back into dictionaries and diffed 157\,204 of them to rediscover the
split the builder already knew. Roughly 78\,\% of the tokens existed only to be
discarded. \texttt{ImmobileCases} now holds the base and the \texttt{y0} array
and formats only what varies.

\paragraph{Key order is the protocol.} \texttt{collect\_solver\_args}
\emph{already} emits \texttt{y0\_0..y0\_18}, so a case dictionary overwrites
them \textbf{in place}: the order is the base's, not base-then-\texttt{y0}. A
first attempt guarded against that and fell back --- silently doing nothing, and
profiling identically. Byte-identity is asserted at $n = 1, 2, 500, 5000$, on an
all-identical batch (the empty-delta guard) and on a sliced shard.

\section{The fast step}

\subsection{Per-phase scaling, and the first retraction}
\label{sec:retract}
Whole-solver thread scaling is poor ($1.68\times$ from 1 to 40 threads at
\SI{200}{\nano\meter}, a 7\,\% spread at \SI{1000}{\nano\meter}) and worst at
the inherited default of 80. Resolving it \emph{by phase} settles where the
serial time is:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
threads & assembly & ``precond'' & krylov & total\\
\midrule
1 & 6.03 & 3.48 & 6.97 & 16.52\\
4 & 4.14 & 3.45 & 3.67 & 11.30\\
20 & 3.72 & 3.83 & 2.63 & 10.23\\
\midrule
scaling & $1.62\times$ & \cellcolor{red!15}$\mathbf{0.91\times}$ & $2.65\times$ & $1.61\times$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Retraction 1.} An earlier revision asserted FE assembly was serial
and made ``parallelize assembly'' priority 2. \textbf{Wrong.}
\texttt{BilinearWeakForm::globalTriplets} is already parallel, over explicit
contiguous chunks concatenated in chunk order precisely so the triplet
sequence stays bit-identical to the serial one. It scales poorly
($1.62\times$), but poor is not absent. Krylov, expected to be the bottleneck,
is the \emph{best}-scaling phase.

\subsection{Fix 3: cache the sparsity pattern}
The dominant phase is not preconditioning: the reaction solver uses
\texttt{use\_directSolver = false}, so its preconditioner is Eigen's diagonal
one. The timer brackets \texttt{computeFromTriplets}, i.e.\ sparse-matrix
\emph{construction} --- \texttt{setFromTriplets} on $A$, a pass to strike out
the Dirichlet rows and columns, then \texttt{setFromTriplets} on $A_1$, twice
per Newton iteration and serial in Eigen.

Across the Newton loop that pattern is invariant; only values move. The first
call records \texttt{aSlot} (triplet $\rightarrow$ slot in $A$) and
\texttt{a1FromA} (slot in $A$ $\rightarrow$ slot in $A_1$) by binary search
inside each compressed row, $O(\mathrm{nnz}\log\mathrm{nnz})$ once; later calls
zero the value arrays and accumulate in place, in triplet order --- the order
\texttt{setFromTriplets} sums duplicates in --- so the matrix is bit-identical.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\SI{200}{\nano\meter}, per fast solve & baseline & pattern cache & gain\\
\midrule
``precond'' & \SI{4.758}{\second} & \SI{2.885}{\second} & $1.65\times$\\
solve total & \SI{10.378}{\second} & \SI{8.095}{\second} & $1.28\times$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Convergence unchanged to every printed digit (4 iterations,
$c_{\mathrm{Error}} = 8.785110566792336\times10^{-7}$). At
\SI{1000}{\nano\meter} the invocation falls \SI{476.0}{\second} $\rightarrow$
\SI{415.6}{\second}.

\subsection{Fix 4: stop oversubscribing}
\texttt{qssa.py} launched \texttt{DDomp} through \texttt{wsl.exe} with no
environment, so it inherited 80 threads. It now sets
\texttt{OMP\_NUM\_THREADS=20} and forwards it via \texttt{WSLENV}, which WSL
does not otherwise inherit.

\label{sec:retract2}

\paragraph{Retraction 2.} Earlier revisions attributed the gap to ``mesh
reading, trial-function construction, FE data structures and \texttt{evl}
output.'' \textbf{Mesh handling is 3\,\%.} The cost is
\texttt{mSolver.compute(mBWF)} --- a \texttt{CholmodSupernodalLLT}
factorization of the diffusion operator, \textbf{larger than the entire
iterative solve} and growing as $N^{1.86}$ against the solve's $N^{1.33}$, so
its share rises with every increase in domain size. Its companion in
\texttt{initializeSolver}, the Dirichlet BC assignment, is
\SI{0.014}{\second} --- nothing.

It is also performed to serve \emph{one} diffusion solve of
\SI{0.93}{\second}, on a matrix that depends only on the mesh and the diffusion
tensor and is therefore identical across all 64 invocations of a
\SI{40}{dpa} march.

\subsection{Fix 5: a resident DDomp}
\texttt{DDomp} gains an opt-in \texttt{--server} mode: mesh, trial functions,
\texttt{DefectiveCrystal} and the factorization are built once, and each
\texttt{SOLVE} line on stdin re-reads the configuration and re-solves.
\texttt{initializeConfiguration} assigns only the CD fields, and
\texttt{initializeSolver} is guarded by \texttt{solverInitialized}, so the
numerics are untouched. Without \texttt{--server} the path is byte-for-byte the
original; \texttt{DISLOCLUSTER\_DDOMP\_SERVER=0} restores it from the driver
side.

\paragraph{Two pieces of carried state, and why they matter more than the
speedup.} A resident solver is only worth having if it is exactly equivalent,
and two things were not:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \texttt{runSteps()} advances \texttt{runID} \emph{and} does
        \texttt{totalTime += dt}, with \texttt{ClusterDynamics} taking
        \texttt{lastUpdateTime} from \texttt{totalTime}. Restoring
        \texttt{runID} alone leaves cycle 2 doing nothing and silently
        returning cycle 1's answer.
  \item \texttt{mobileClustersIncrement} is zeroed in the \textbf{constructor
        only}, and the first Newton assembly of every cycle reads it
        (\texttt{bWF\_R1c}, \texttt{bWF\_RIc}). Left over, it perturbed the
        result by \textbf{2.3e-9} --- small, deterministic, and invisible to any
        tolerance check. It is now zeroed per configuration, a no-op for a
        one-shot run.
\end{enumerate}

Both were found only because the acceptance test is bit-identity rather than
agreement. The first two attempts looked correct and were not.

\section{End-to-end result}
A \SI{200}{\nano\meter} hexagonal march to \SI{1}{dpa} (doses $0.01$, $0.1$,
$1.0$; 5 substeps per interval; \texttt{fem\_every} $=5$), measured against the
\emph{true} pre-optimization code --- no sharding, 80 threads, pre-optimization
\texttt{DDomp}:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
 & original & optimized & speedup\\
\midrule
fast solve, 1st (builds everything) & \SI{28.1}{\second} & \SI{21.5}{\second} & $1.31\times$\\
fast solve, steady state & \SI{28.1}{\second} & \textbf{\SI{12.1}{\second}} & $\mathbf{2.32\times}$\\
slow step (sum of three intervals) & \SI{67.0}{\second} & \SI{28.0}{\second} & $2.39\times$\\
\rowcolor{green!12}
\textbf{march wall} & \SI{161.7}{\second} & \textbf{\SI{84.1}{\second}} & $\mathbf{1.92\times}$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\texttt{march\_state.npz} is \textbf{bit-identical} to the original: max
absolute difference $0.000\times10^{0}$ over $4\times21\,052\times19$ values.

An earlier baseline in this note quoted $1.22\times$; that baseline already
contained sharding and the thread fix and so understated the total. The figure
above is against code with every optimization removed.

\section{What remains}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The unexplained in-solve residual} --- \SI{28.96}{\second} at
        \SI{1000}{\nano\meter} against \SI{0.26}{\second} at
        \SI{200}{\nano\meter}, $N^{2.14}$: the steepest term in the table and
        entirely unidentified. It is now the obvious next profiling target.
  \item \textbf{Mesh creation} --- \SI{11.4}{\second} at
        \SI{1000}{\nano\meter}, amortized by the resident solver but still paid
        once, and emitted as 129\,569 progress lines.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{dedup\_rtol}} --- $10^{-8}$ against an integrator
        \texttt{rtol} of $10^{-6}$; dedup measures $\times1.21$. Loosening it
        cuts slow work proportionally, with error bounded by a tolerance
        already accepted. Untested, and the cheapest item here.
  \item \textbf{Node count} --- the largest cost driver: the fast solve scales
        $N^{1.33}$ and the boundary layer is 125\,k of the
        \SI{1000}{\nano\meter} mesh's 130\,k tets. A modelling decision, not an
        optimization.
  \item \textbf{Making \texttt{mSolver} iterative} --- would remove the
        Cholesky outright rather than amortizing it, but changes the Newton
        loop's starting iterate and so cannot be bit-identical. Deliberately
        not taken.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
\texttt{fem\_every} is excluded on purpose: splitting error is first order in
the fast-solve spacing, so time saved there comes out of the physics.

\section*{Reproducing}
\begin{lstlisting}
DISLOCLUSTER_SOLVER_SHARDS=1     # one solver process per batch (pre-Fix-1)
DISLOCLUSTER_DDOMP_THREADS=20    # fast-step thread count
DISLOCLUSTER_DDOMP_SERVER=0      # one DDomp process per solve (pre-Fix-5)
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Caveat.} Every number is from one host. The processor-group limit is
Windows-specific, the shard plan is sized from \texttt{os.cpu\_count()}, and the
fast-step thread optimum shifted between the two problem sizes measured. Single
stage timings vary by up to $\sim$25\,\% here, so the per-phase tables support
which phase dominates, not their third digit. The end-to-end
\SI{200}{\nano\meter} figure is a direct measurement; the corresponding
\SI{1000}{\nano\meter} figure is not yet, and the fast step's share is larger
there (77\,\% against $\sim$45\,\%), so the totals will differ.
